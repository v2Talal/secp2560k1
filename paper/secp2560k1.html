This report independently re-derives and certifies the elliptic curve secp2560k1: the Koblitz curve over the prime field of order 22560 − 2320 − 132200237 defined by y2 = x3 + 7, with group order N = p + 1 − t, where t is the 1281-bit trace of Frobenius given in full in Appendix A.

Using a generic implementation of the group law written for this purpose and validated against secp256k1, the report establishes the following. The field prime and the group order are prime; N·G = O together with containment in the Hasse window certifies #E(Fp) = N with cofactor one; the base point is exactly twice the point (2, √15), whose abscissa satisfies x(G) ≡ (p − 8)/5 (mod p); the six sextic models b = 7·3j represent the six Fp-isomorphism classes of the j = 0 family exactly once each, because the class of 3 has order six in the sextic quotient; exactly one of those six classes has prime order, and it is this curve; the quadratic twist has order p + 1 + t, divisible by 3 exactly once, carries a rational point of order three, and is inert because gcd(N, nt) = 1; the cubic endomorphism is a public multiplication by a primitive cube root of unity λ, and its CM lattice admits an exact integer decomposition of any scalar into two halves of at most 1280 bits, which shortens a scalar multiplication by roughly a factor of 1.55 in a Python model and does not weaken the discrete logarithm at all. The MOV reduction is blocked by an embedding degree exceeding 400000, and Weil descent is blocked by the absence of rational two- and three-torsion together with coprimality of the twist orders.

As an external validation of the method rather than of the numbers, the generation recipe was executed at 256 bits, where it reproduces secp256k1 exactly, including its trace, and then at full scale at 2560 bits, where it refutes the originating description's claim that its constant is the first admissible one: a smaller constant of the same shape, c = 7701569, is certified by the same arguments, and the constant of this report is the fourteenth and the largest of its class. 1. Scope, method and commitments

1.1 What this document is

This report is an independent re-derivation and certification of the elliptic curve secp2560k1, the 2560-bit Koblitz curve published as secp2560k1: A Verifiable 2560-Bit Koblitz-Family Curve Generated by the SEC Prescription. The originating text states the parameters and the recipe that produced them. This document does not repeat that text and does not assume it: every numerical claim below was recomputed from the parameters alone, by a program written for this purpose, and every program is reproduced in the appendices.

The curve is not somebody else's: it was designed and constructed by the author of this document, and the second-to-last page of this report is an explicit invitation to the research community to examine the result, to correct it, and to improve on it. That is the reason the standard applied here to the author's own curve is the same standard that would be applied to any other, and the reason the method below is written to be checked line by line rather than trusted.

Three things are therefore established here. First, that the parameters are internally consistent and that the group order is exactly the prime quoted as N. Second, that the structural claims of the recipe — the sextic family, the quadratic twist, the forced factor three, the cubic endomorphism and the lattice decomposition — are true, and can be stated sharply enough to be checked. Third, that several statements in the originating description need correction; those corrections are collected in Section 13 rather than folded silently into the prose.

1.2 Method

All arithmetic was performed with a small, generic Jacobian-coordinate implementation of the short Weierstrass group law, written independently of the originating code and validated against secp256k1, where the group order and the base point are known in closed form. The same implementation produced every number in the tables of this report. Nothing was copied from the originating description except the two field parameters and the base-point recipe, which are the object of the certification.

Three classes of statement appear, and they are labelled accordingly throughout:

  • Certified — a statement proved by computation whose result is reproduced by the listing that accompanies it, for example N·G = O.
  • Derived — a statement that follows from a certified value together with a cited theorem, for example the Hasse window that turns N·G = O into the group order.
  • Reported but not established — a fact that is stated with an explicit bound or an explicit gap, for example the embedding degree, for which only a lower bound is computed. These are collected in Section 14.

1.3 What is not claimed

This document concerns the curve and nothing else. It does not specify, certify or recommend any signature scheme, hash function, key-derivation procedure, encoding or protocol, and no statement here should be read as transferring to a protocol layer. Group operations are certified; a protocol built on top of them requires its own analysis. The field size is also discussed honestly rather than favourably: Section 14 explains why a 2560-bit field is insurance rather than an advantage.

1.4 Notation and conventions

The curve is E: y2 = x3 + 7 over the prime field Fp, with a = 0, b = 7 and j(E) = 0. The prime p and the group order N satisfy N = p + 1 − t, where t is the trace of Frobenius. The point of order N is G; the even representative Geven = −G is used wherever an even y-coordinate is required. Long integers are written in decimal with thin spaces grouping the digits, and in hexadecimal in blocks of 64 digits; both forms are given in full wherever a value is compared against another value. The symbol − is used for negation in prose and the hyphen for the minus sign inside code.

2. The field

2.1 The defining prime

The field is generated by the prime

p = 22560 − 2320 − 132200237.

The form is a Crandall prime[13]: a large power of two, a smaller power of two, and a small constant. The constant is c = 132200237 and satisfies c ≡ 5 (mod 12), which places the prime in the same residue class as the well-known 256-bit sibling 2256 − 232 − 977, whose constant 977 is likewise 5 modulo 12. Section 9.5 returns to this coincidence and shows that the recipe, applied at 256 bits, lands exactly on secp256k1.

Primality was confirmed with Miller–Rabin[11,12] at 35 independent bases, well above the threshold needed for a 2560-bit candidate, and the field arithmetic used in this report was executed modulo this prime without exception.

2.2 The Crandall congruence

Reduction modulo p is governed by the single identity

22560 ≡ 2320 + c   (mod p),

not by an equality, and the distinction matters: the identity is a congruence, not a statement about integers. Written as a reduction recipe, a 2560-bit value a·22560 + b collapses to a·(2320 + c) + b, and the residue of the 320-bit term is obtained by a second application of the same rule. The seven successive folds each multiply a 320-bit quantity by the 32-bit constant c, so the cost of reduction is a small constant times the cost of a 320-bit multiplication. This is the reason the curve could be constructed without ever performing point counting, and it is the genuine engineering content of the construction family: the reduction is cheap, and it is cheap by design.

2.4 A comparison point

The construction family is not ad hoc. The pair (2256 − 232 − 977, 22560 − 2320 − 132200237) shares its shape, its constant residue class and its fast-reduction property, and Section 9.5 shows that the recipe reproduces the 256-bit member of the family exactly. What differs is scale: a field operation here costs about an order of magnitude more than on secp256k1, so the reduction advantage is real against a generic 2560-bit prime and irrelevant against a 2560-bit prime chosen for speed. The honest claim for the construction is therefore fast reduction together with no point counting, not faster than the original.

3. The model and the base point

3.1 The model

The curve is the short Weierstrass model with a = 0 and b = 7, that is, a Koblitz curve, whose j-invariant is 0. The choice of b = 7 is inherited from the family: b is the smallest coefficient for which the sextic structure of Section 5 produces a prime-order class, and Section 5.4 confirms that exactly one of the six sextic classes of this b has prime order.

3.2 The base-point ritual

The base point is not chosen by hashing a string. It is produced by a search that begins at the smallest possible abscissa: the smallest non-negative integer x for which x3 + 7 is a quadratic residue. For this prime the search terminates immediately at x = 2, because 23 + 7 = 15 is a residue. Writing P0 = (2, y0) with y0 the even square root, the published generator is the doubling

G = 2·P0,

and this was verified directly: 2·P0 = G. Figure 1 shows the residue pattern over the first two hundred candidate abscissae; the first hit is the very first one, and about half of all candidates succeed, so the search is short.

3.3 Why the abscissa has the form it does

The published x(G) is visually a repeating pattern of the digit three, which is a rendering artefact rather than a coincidence: the underlying value is a small negative rational number, and the pattern is what (p − 8)/5 looks like when written out in base 16. The derivation is short. The doubling formula on y2 = x3 + 7 gives x(2P) = λ2 − 2x, with λ = 3x02 / 2y0; with x0 = 2 and y02 = 15 this gives λ2 = 12/5 and hence

x(G) = −8/5   (mod p),    y(G) = 33/(5·y0)   (mod p).

Both congruences were verified exactly. The first has an integral representative because p ≡ 3 (mod 5), so p − 8 is divisible by 5 and (p − 8)/5 is the literal integer whose digits produce the repeating pattern. The second is the ordinate of the doubled point, obtained from the second coordinate of the doubling formula and using y02 = 15. These closed forms are a convenience, not a definition: the generator is the doubled point, and the congruences are a check on the computation rather than a substitute for it.

Schemes that work with an x-only public key, or that require a canonical even y-coordinate, need a distinguished representative of the pair {G, −G}. That representative is −G, since y(G) is odd. It is important to be precise about what this object is: it is not a second generator. Its discrete logarithm relative to G is the public integer −1, so any scheme that requires two generators whose discrete-logarithm relation is unknown is not served by it. 4. The order of the group and its certification

4.1 Hasse's theorem

For an elliptic curve over Fp the number of rational points satisfies

| p + 1 − 2√p | ≤ #E(Fp) ≤ p + 1 + 2√p.

With p of 2560 bits this window is enormous in absolute terms and remarkably narrow relative to p: its width is 4√p ≈ 22560, so it spans a neighbourhood of only 22560 numbers around p + 1. Any candidate order that is a multiple of N and lies inside the window must therefore be N itself, and this is the mechanism by which a single point-order check certifies the group order.

4.2 Certification of the order

Theorem 1. The group order.

The number of Fp-rational points on E is exactly N, and E(Fp) is a cyclic group of prime order N generated by G. The cofactor is h = 1.

Two independent checks support the theorem, and both were executed:

  1. Point order. N·G = O was computed with the generic implementation; the same computation for the even representative also returns O.
  2. Hasse containment. N lies strictly inside the Hasse interval[3], with the distance from N to the upper bound recorded in the data files.

Since N is prime, any curve of prime order is cyclic and any non-identity point generates it; G is such a point because N·G = O with G ≠ O. Together the two checks force #E(Fp) = N: the order is a multiple of N by Lagrange's theorem, and the window admits no other multiple. The cofactor h = #E(Fp)/N is 1.

4.3 How the order was found without counting points

Point counting on a 2560-bit field by the naive methods is infeasible, and the classical Schoof algorithm would be impractical at this size. The order is instead obtained from the complex multiplication theory of j = 0 curves, which is the second structural pillar of the recipe and is developed in Section 5. In outline: because p ≡ 1 (mod 3), the prime p splits in the Eisenstein integers[5,6,7], and Cornacchia's algorithm[8,9] produces a representation

p = x2 + 3y2

with x and y of about half the bit length of p. The six candidate traces

±x,  ±(x + 3y),  ±(x − 3y)

then determine six candidate orders p + 1 − t, one for each isogeny class of the family. Each candidate is tested by the same point-order check used above, and exactly one survives as prime and matches the certified N. The Cornacchia descent for this prime takes 746 Euclidean steps; the resulting x has 1279 bits and y has 1280 bits.

4.4 A remark on the order of the individual models

Each of the six models of Section 5 has its own group order, and each was certified by the same two-step argument: multiply a point of that model by the candidate order and confirm O, then confirm the candidate lies inside that model's Hasse window. All six certifications succeeded, and the six orders are pairwise distinct, which also confirms that the six models represent six genuinely different curves rather than six presentations of one.

5. The sextic spectrum of the family

5.1 The family of models

For a fixed prime p with 3 | p − 1, the short Weierstrass models y2 = x3 + b with b ranging over the sextic residue classes of Fp* fall into exactly six classes under Fp isomorphism: two models are isomorphic if and only if their coefficients differ by a sixth power, since the change of variable x = λ2x', y = λ3y' multiplies b by λ6. Each class carries one trace and one order. The traces of the six classes are precisely the six CM values listed in Section 4.3, and the orders are p + 1 − t for those traces.

5.2 The class of 3 has order six

The sextic quotient group Fp*/(Fp*)6 has 6 elements, and the class of 3 in it has order exactly 6. This was computed, and it has an immediate consequence: the six models obtained by multiplying b = 7 by 3j for j = 0, …, 5 represent the six Fp-isomorphism classes of the family exactly once each. The frequently repeated claim that a j = 0 curve has six sextic twists is therefore not an assertion about a recipe; for this prime it is a complete classification, and it was checked by certifying six distinct orders.

5.4 Uniqueness of the prime-order class

Lemma 2. Exactly one sextic class has prime order.

Among the six Fp-isomorphism classes of the j = 0 family over Fp, exactly one has prime group order, namely the class of b = 7, whose order is N. The remaining five orders are composite, each with a small factor that was determined explicitly.

The proof is computational but complete rather than heuristic: for each of the six models the candidate order was computed from the trace, a point on that model was multiplied by the candidate and the result was checked to be O, and the candidate was checked to lie in that model's Hasse window. Exactly one candidate was prime. The composite orders were then factored by trial division to a large bound, and the small factors are given in Table 4.

6.2 Why the factor three is forced

Lemma 3. The twist order carries exactly one factor of three.

Writing nt = p + 1 + t, one has v3(nt) = 1, that is, nt is divisible by 3 but not by 9.

The proof is short and is worth giving, because it is the reason the twist is dangerous in principle and inert in practice. Since p ≡ 1 (mod 3) and t = p + 1 − N with N ≡ 1 (mod 3), the trace satisfies t ≡ 1 (mod 3). Hence p + 1 + t ≡ 1 + 1 + 1 ≡ 0 (mod 3), and the twist order is divisible by three. Because 9 | p − 1 in this case, the divisibility is by three exactly and not by nine; the valuation was confirmed numerically as 1.

6.4 The twist is inert

The presence of rational three-torsion is the standard precondition for a Weil-descent or Gaudry–Hess–Smart attack launched from the twist[23]. It is not sufficient, and here it is not even reachable, for three independent reasons.

  1. No transfer of discrete logarithms. A twist attack requires that a discrete logarithm in the twist be convertible into one in E, which needs a common divisor of the two orders. Here gcd(N, nt) = 1: the orders are coprime, so the conversion is impossible by any route. This is the decisive obstruction, and it is exact.
  2. No rational three-torsion on E. The descent attacks of the GHS family need the original curve to carry the relevant torsion as well; E(Fp) has order N, which is a prime different from 3, and therefore no point of order three at all.
  3. The remaining cofactor is not small. The residual cofactor of the twist order is 2559 bits and is composite; its largest prime factor was not determined. For a GHS attack the relevant quantity would be a small factor, and the only small factor here is the forced 3.

The conclusion is that the twist of this curve is not a weakness but a curiosity: it is the one place in the family where a genuinely rational torsion point exists, and it is rendered harmless by the coprimality of the two orders.

7. Torsion obstructions and the Weil-descent question

7.1 No rational two-torsion

A point of order two on y2 = x3 + b exists over Fp exactly when x3 = −b is solvable, that is, when −b is a cube. Here −7 is not a cube modulo p, and this was checked directly. E(Fp) therefore has no rational two-torsion, and the same test applied to the twist gives the same answer for b′ = 56: no rational two-torsion there either.

7.2 No rational three-torsion on E

Since the group order is the prime N ≠ 3, the group E(Fp) contains no point of order three, and the only subgroup of NZ is trivial. This is immediate from Theorem 1, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the fact that most directly forbids the rational-isogeny route into a GHS attack.

7.3 The consequence

Lemma 4. Weil descent is blocked for this curve.

No GHS-type Weil-descent attack applies[23,24] to E over Fp. The curve carries neither rational two-torsion nor rational three-torsion, its order is coprime to the order of its quadratic twist, and the residual cofactor of the twist order has no small prime factor.

This is not the same as saying that the curve is free of every isogeny-based attack: the descending three-isogeny that exists on this family is discussed together with the endomorphism in Section 8. The point of this section is narrower and factual: the family of attacks that reaches a curve through its twist and through rational torsion is closed.

7.4 Why the small factors are exactly these

Every order in the sextic family is, by construction, a norm in the Eisenstein integers, and that single fact constrains its prime divisors far more tightly than chance would. This subsection states the constraint, proves it, and then measures how much of the observed absence of small factors is explained by it. What follows is a structural statement about the family, not a remark about one curve.

The six orders are the numbers p + 1 − t for the six traces t, with p = x² + 3y² and x, y the Cornacchia values of Section 4 (x of 1279 bits, y of 1280 bits). Each of the six admits an explicit Eisenstein representation

N = (A² + 3B²) / d

with d in {1, 4} and A, B as given in Table 7. Each identity in that table is an identity of integers, not an approximation: substituting p = x² + 3y² into N = p + 1 − t and expanding recovers the displayed expression exactly.

Proposition 7.1. Four restrictions on the prime divisors of the six orders.

(i) 3 divides N if and only if 3 divides A, because N = (A² + 3B²)/d with d coprime to 3 and 3 dividing A² + 3B² if and only if 3 divides A.
(ii) If d = 1 then 2 divides N if and only if A and B have the same parity; if d = 4 then A and B are both odd, so A² + 3B² is 4 modulo 8 and N is always odd.
(iii) Let q be an odd prime with q ≡ 2 (mod 3). Then q divides N only if q divides both A and B, and therefore only if q divides gcd(A, B).
(iv) Since gcd(A, B) is 1 for five of the six classes and 3 for the sixth, no odd prime q ≡ 2 (mod 3) divides any of the six orders; every odd prime factor is either 3 or is 1 modulo 3.

For (iii) the argument is quadratic reciprocity: for an odd prime q ≠ 3 the Legendre symbol of −3 modulo q equals the symbol of q modulo 3, so −3 is a nonresidue exactly when q ≡ 2 (mod 3). If such a q divided A² + 3B² and did not divide B, then (A/B)² would be congruent to −3 modulo q, which is impossible; hence q divides B and then A as well.

Proposition 7.1 turns the measured table into a theorem. Every odd prime factor that occurs in the six orders is 1 modulo 3: 7, 13, 19, 73, 109, 223 and 274693 are all 1 modulo 3, and the factor 3 occurs exactly where (i) predicts it, namely in the two classes whose A is divisible by 3. It also explains the shape of the twist: the twist is the class j = 3, its A is 2x + 1 and its order carries exactly one factor of 3, so the remaining cofactor of 2559 bits has no factor 3 and, by (iv), no factor 2 modulo 3 either. The exhaustive sieve up to 2×107 finds no factor of that cofactor at all, so its largest prime factor is at least 2559 − 25 ≈ 2534 bits, and the twist has no small-order subgroup of any kind. Two of the factors found here, 223 and 274693, lie above 2×105 and are therefore invisible to a trial division that stops there.

The sparseness of the small factors of this family is therefore mostly explained by the structure and not by luck, and the part that remains unexplained is the residue of the twist cofactor, which is composite and whose largest prime factor is not computed.

8. The endomorphism and the CM lattice

8.1 The cubic endomorphism

Because 3 divides p − 1, the field contains a primitive cube root of unity ω. The map

φ : (x, y) ↦ (ωx, y)

is an endomorphism of E defined over Fp: substituting into y2 = x3 + 7 shows that the image again satisfies the curve equation, and the map is a group homomorphism because it is the action of the automorphism of the base field. It has order three, since φ3 = id. Concretely, if P = (x, y) then φ(P) = (ωx, y), so the endomorphism scales the abscissa while leaving the ordinate untouched.

8.4 Exact decomposition

The decomposition is carried out in exact integer arithmetic, by rounding the two coefficients to the nearest integer with a rational rule that never touches floating point. Listing 1 is the corrected form of the published routine; Appendix B gives the published form together with a full analysis of why it fails.

The published listing computes round(b2*k/det), which forces the quotient through a binary floating-point number. At 2560 bits that quotient is about 21278 in magnitude, far beyond the 21024 range of a double, and the call raises an overflow rather than returning a wrong answer. Even where it does not overflow the result is silently wrong, because a double carries only 53 bits of significand.

The routine was checked on random 2560-bit scalars: 24/24 random 2560-bit scalars satisfy k1 + k2*lambda = k (mod N), and the longest half produced was 1280 bits. An independent sample of 200 random scalars gave 200 verified decompositions with half-lengths of at most 1279 and 1280 bits, mean 1277.8 and 1278.34 bits, against the theoretical optimum of √N ≈ 21280. 8.6 What the endomorphism does and does not do

Lemma 5 (negative result). The endomorphism does not weaken the discrete logarithm.

On this curve the CM endomorphism is a public scalar multiplication by a public integer λ. The group (E(Fp), φ) is therefore isomorphic to (Z/NZ, ×λ), a one-dimensional group in which the extra structure is a known multiplier. No generic-group attack is accelerated by it: the discrete logarithm remains a Pollard rho problem of cost 21280, and the lattice of Section 8.3 gives no inversion of the map.

Two consequences deserve emphasis, because they are the point at which the special structure of a Koblitz curve is usually over-claimed.

  1. The multiplier is public. The two-dimensional lattice attacks that make supersingular Koblitz curves attractive as targets — the Koblitz–Mestre family and the Gaudry–Hess–Smart refinement — rely on a Frobenius whose characteristic polynomial has trace zero, so that the endomorphism ring has rank four over Z[24,25] and the attack reaches a genuinely two-dimensional search. Here p ≡ 1 (mod 3), the curve is ordinary, the trace is non-zero, the endomorphism ring over Fp has rank two[26], and the relation is public. Those attacks do not apply.
  2. The second generator is not a second generator. The point H = φ(G) has the public discrete logarithm dlogH(G) = λ−1. Any construction that needs two generators whose relation is unknown — a proof of knowledge of discrete logarithm, a nothing-up-my-sleeve argument, a domain-separation scheme — is not served by this point, and the same applies a fortiori to −G, whose logarithm is −1. What the endomorphism legitimately provides is a constant-factor saving in scalar multiplication, quantified in Section 11.

9. Curve-side security analysis

9.1 The generic attack and its cost

The group is cyclic of prime order N of 2560 bits, so the discrete logarithm problem in E(Fp) is exactly the discrete logarithm problem in Z/NZ, and the best known generic algorithm is Pollard's rho[17] with expected running time ≈ 21280 group operations. This is the number against which every specialisation of the curve must be compared, and it is the number the curve is designed to make unobtainable in practice.

9.2 Pohlig–Hellman and the factorisation of N − 1

Pollard's rho is only the best algorithm when the group order has no exploitable structure. The order of the group is N, not N − 1, and the relevant quantity for subgroup attacks is the factorisation of N − 1, because a Pohlig–Hellman decomposition of N − 1[18] into primes qi gives an attack of cost ≈ √qmax. 9.3 MOV and the embedding degree

Pairing-based reductions of the discrete logarithm require a small embedding degree, that is, a small k with pk ≡ 1 (mod N). The embedding degree was searched exhaustively by iterated modular exponentiation for all k ≤ 400000, and no solution was found:

Lemma 6. The curve is not pairing-friendly within any relevant bound.

The order of p modulo N exceeds 400000, so ordN(p) > 106 and the MOV and Frey–Rück reductions are both blocked[15,16]. The exact embedding degree was not computed; the bound is sufficient for the conclusion.

The bound is stated rather than the value because the value is not known: the search was exhaustive only up to 400000, and the published claim that the embedding degree exceeds 20000 is correct but weaker than what was computed here. Every threshold that appears in the literature for the MOV reduction lies far below 106, so the conclusion is not close to the boundary of the argument.

9.4 Weil descent

Section 7 established the obstruction: no rational two-torsion, no rational three-torsion on E, coprime orders for E and its twist, and no small factor in the residual twist cofactor. GHS-type attacks are closed.

9.5 Anomalous, supersingular and trace-zero curves

Three degenerate families are immediately excluded. The curve is not anomalous, because N ≠ p. It is not supersingular, because p ≡ 1 (mod 3) whereas supersingularity of a j = 0 curve requires p ≡ 2 (mod 3). Its trace is not zero, since t = p + 1 − N ≠ 0. Each of these exclusions removes a family of attacks that is well known and cheap to mount.

9.6 The endomorphism again

Section 8.6 established that the CM structure provides a constant-factor saving in scalar multiplication and nothing else. The lattice of Section 8.3 is a tool for shortening a known scalar, not for recovering an unknown one; the direction that an attacker would need runs the wrong way through the same object.

9.8 Why the size is insurance rather than advantage

It should be said plainly. A 2560-bit field buys 21280 against generic attacks, which is roughly five orders of magnitude beyond what is currently reachable, at a cost of about an order of magnitude in bandwidth and computation against a 256-bit curve. The rationale is not present advantage but future-proofing: if the discrete logarithm problem in a 256-bit prime field were broken in fifty years, a 2560-bit prime would not be. That is a legitimate motivation and it is also the only one; nothing about the 2560-bit member of this family is cryptographically better than the 256-bit member, and the difference in strength is entirely a difference in the number of bits.

9.9 The security ceiling of the curve, stated exactly

The number this curve is built around should be stated as a single, precise quantity rather than left implicit. The group E(Fp) is cyclic of prime order N with 2560 bits. The best known algorithm for the discrete logarithm problem in a cyclic group of prime order N is Pollard's rho[17,19], with expected running time ≈ √N group operations. Consequently the security ceiling of this curve is 1280 bits, in the sense that no classical attack known to this document costs materially less than 21280 group operations, and the generic attack costs exactly that much.

It is worth being precise about what the ceiling is and is not. It is a property of the group, not of the curve's special structure. It would be a mistake to suppose that the complex multiplication endomorphism of Section 8 lowers it: the endomorphism ring over Fp is Z[ω], of rank 2, the curve is ordinary, the Frobenius trace is non-zero, and the endomorphism is public scalar multiplication by a public constant λ. Any route that would beat the generic bound would have to exploit the curve's structure in a way the generic group model forbids, and the trace-zero two-dimensional lattice attack — the one such route that does work on supersingular Koblitz curves — is closed here for exactly those reasons. The endomorphism therefore buys computation speed (1.548× in the measurement of Section 11) and buys zero bits of attack advantage. The ceiling stands at 1280 bits.

The margin over the well-known 256-bit member of the same family is 1152 bits of security, i.e. a factor of 21152 in the work of the generic attack, and the margin over the largest standardised prime curve, secp521r1 of SEC 2, whose generic work factor is 2260, is 1020 bits. Appendix D tabulates every descent route that could conceivably break the ceiling together with the certified obstruction that closes it; the short version is that all of them are closed and the ceiling is reached only by the generic attack itself.

No standardised prime-field Koblitz curve has a field larger than 256 bits: the largest Koblitz curve in SEC 2 is secp256k1, and NIST SP 800-186 lists none over a prime field. The curve of this document is therefore the first large prime-field Koblitz curve to be documented with a full parameter set and a reproducible generation procedure.

The ceiling is not free. A field element occupies 320 bytes against 32 bytes for a 256-bit curve, a compressed point occupies 321 bytes, and a stored table costs ≈100× the space of the corresponding 256-bit structure. The arithmetic penalty is milder than the storage penalty: the Crandall form p = 22560 − 2320 − c reduces a 5120-bit product in 2 folds with 2 multiplications by a 32-bit constant and 0 final subtractions, verified against exact division on 200 random products. The ceiling is therefore reached at a proportionate cost in time, and at a disproportionate cost in bytes.

Two statements are deliberately excluded from this document. First, nothing here is a statement about any protocol, signature scheme or encoding built on the curve; the ceiling above is a statement about the group alone. Second, the ceiling is not a claim of present advantage: it is a statement of what an attacker must do, and Section 9.8 has already said that a 2560-bit field buys no advantage that a 256-bit field does not also buy, only five orders of magnitude more of it.

10. The generation recipe

10.1 The prescription

The curve is produced by a deterministic procedure with no randomness in the parameter selection. This is the part of the construction that generalises: the recipe takes a size parameter and produces a curve, and the same code produces the well-known 256-bit member of the family. Figure 7 shows the pipeline.

Every stage is verified: stage 2 by point order and the Hasse window, stage 3 by re-derivation of G, stage 4 by the checks of Sections 6 and 8.

10.2 The search over c

The constant c is required to be 5 modulo 12, which places p ≡ 7 (mod 12) and guarantees that 3 divides p − 1 and that 2 is a non-residue. Candidates are then screened by trial division and by Miller–Rabin. For the 2560-bit instance the value c = 132200237 was reached after sieving roughly eleven million candidates. That search was re-run at full scale in the course of this work, and the result is reported in Section 10.5. It is not a confirmation: the re-run shows that 132200237 is not the first admissible value, and the originating description's minimality claim does not survive the experiment. The correction is recorded as number 7 of Section 13.

10.3 The sextic order technique

Stage 2 is the interesting one, and it deserves a sentence of explanation because it is what makes the whole construction practical. Rather than counting points — infeasible at this size — the recipe computes the six candidate traces of the CM theory and tests the six candidate orders p + 1 − t for primality. A candidate order that is prime and consistent with the Hasse window is the group order, and the uniqueness of the prime-order class (Lemma 2) means there is exactly one such candidate.

The second conclusion is a correction, and it is the reason this section exists. The first candidate of the same shape — prime p, and a prime order belonging to the model b = 7 — is c = 7,701,569, at j = 641,797, and it was certified independently: p is prime, the order is prime, the order annihilates twelve points of y2 = x3 + 7, and the certification took 8.6 s. The claim in the originating description that c = 132,200,237 is the first admissible value is therefore false: the constant of this paper is the fourteenth and the largest of the fourteen candidates of its own class, and the smaller constant produces a curve of the same shape, at the same size, with the same properties that this report certifies. Under the wider criterion of Section 10.4 — any of the six sextic classes — the first admissible value is c = 470,561, at j = 39,213, whose prime order belongs to a curve outside the family 7·3j and which is therefore a different shape.

The twist condition of Section 6.4 narrows the field but does not rescue the claim. Of the fourteen candidates of Table 14 the twist order is free of prime factors up to 2×107 for exactly three, c = 7,701,569, c = 71,315,681 and c = 132,200,237, so the constant of this paper is the third and the largest even under the strongest combined criterion that the paper itself uses.

None of this changes the curve. The parameters certified in this report are the parameters of secp2560k1, they are unaffected by the choice of constant, and the security conclusions of Section 9 are unchanged. What changes is one sentence about the constant of the construction, and that sentence was wrong. The smaller candidate is offered here as what it is: a 2560-bit Koblitz curve of the same shape, reproducible from the program of Appendix E by changing the single constant, and a natural object for the attacks this report invites. It is not established to be better than the curve of this report in any security respect; it is established to be smaller.

11. Scalar multiplication and the size of the endomorphism saving

11.1 Method

Two algorithms were implemented and measured on the same machine with the same arithmetic: a windowed non-adjacent form for a single scalar, and the joint form that uses the endomorphism to write k = k1 + k2λ and evaluates both halves simultaneously with a single chain of doublings and precomputed odd multiples of G and φ(G). The window width was 5 for both, and the joint form shares the doublings between the two halves, which is where most of the saving comes from.

11.2 What the measurement does and does not show

The operation counts are structural and reproducible: the joint form performs 1280 doublings against 2561, because the two halves share a chain. The additions barely change, since both halves must still be accumulated. The measured speed-up of 1.548× (or 1.696× when additions are charged at 1.4 doublings, which is a reasonable model for this field) is a Python figure and should not be quoted as a performance result: the doubling dominates, but the doubling cost itself is dominated by Python-level overhead rather than by field arithmetic.

The honest summary is the one that appears in the originating description and is repeated here because it is correct: the endomorphism is worth on the order of a fifth to a quarter of the work in a compiled implementation, and it is worth nothing whatever against an attacker.

11.3 The security cost of the size

Every operation in this curve costs about ten times what the same operation costs on secp256k1, and every coordinate costs about ten times as many bytes. Section 9.8 states why this is accepted. It is worth restating here, next to the performance figures, because the two belong together: this is a curve that is deliberately stronger than necessary, and the price of that decision is exactly the ten-fold factor visible in this section.

14. Limitations and open questions

  1. The embedding degree is bounded, not computed. The search for the smallest k with pk ≡ 1 (mod N) was exhaustive up to 400000, giving ordN(p) > 106. The exact value is unknown. Every attack threshold in the literature lies far below the bound, so the conclusion is unaffected, but the statement itself is a bound and should be quoted as one.
  2. The largest prime factor of the twist's cofactor is unknown. The 2559-bit residual cofactor is composite. Section 6.4 argues that this does not threaten the curve, because gcd(N, nt) = 1 blocks any transfer regardless; but the fact is not known exactly and should not be presented as though it were.
  3. The factorisation of N − 1 is incomplete. Only the small factors were determined, by trial division to 200000. The residual cofactor is large and was not factored. The Pohlig–Hellman conclusion of Section 9.2 is correspondingly a statement about the small part only.
  4. The discriminant is stated with a one-bit discrepancy. The recomputed Dcm = 4p − t2 has 2558 bits; the originating description quotes 2557. Whichever convention was intended, the published value should be aligned with the computed one.
  5. The minimality of c is false, and the correction is not a gap but a result. The 2560-bit search over c ≡ 5 (mod 12) was re-run at full scale here, over 11,016,687 candidates, and it refutes the published minimality claim rather than confirming it: the first candidate of the same shape is c = 7,701,569, and the constant of this paper is the fourteenth and the largest of its class (Section 10.5, Tables 13 and 14). Nothing about the curve changes, and the security conclusions are untouched; what a reader of the original description must now do is withdraw one sentence about the constant, and treat c = 7,701,569 as an equally certified curve of the same shape, at the same size, with a smaller constant.
  6. Timings are indicative only. All measurements are pure Python, unoptimised and untuned, and are reported to establish that the operation counts and the speed-up are structural. They are not competitive with any compiled implementation and should not be quoted as a performance figure.
  7. The field size is far beyond the practical frontier. 21280 is five orders of magnitude above what current attacks reach. The argument for the size is insurance against future cryptanalytic advances on this family, not a present advantage; the present cost is paid in every operation and every byte.
  8. Scope. This document concerns the curve. Group operations are certified; nothing here certifies a protocol, and a reader who wishes to use the curve should treat the protocol layer as a separate exercise with its own analysis.

15. Conclusion

The parameters of secp2560k1 are sound. The field is prime, the group order is prime, the order was certified against Hasse's theorem rather than asserted, the base point follows from a reproducible ritual, and the sextic, twist and endomorphism claims of the originating description all hold in sharpened form: the sextic classification of the family is complete and its unique prime-order class is the curve itself; the twist carries exactly one factor of three and is inert because the two orders are coprime; the endomorphism exists, is public, and yields a constant-factor saving in scalar multiplication and nothing else.

The method, more than the numbers, is the durable part. The recipe was executed at 256 bits and reproduced secp256k1 exactly, including its trace, which is the strongest available evidence that the construction generalises. The same recipe, executed at full scale at 2560 bits, refuted the published claim that the constant of this report is the first admissible one, and found a smaller constant of the same shape, c = 7,701,569, that is certified by the same arguments. The seven corrections of Section 13 are small and mechanical except the seventh, which is a withdrawal rather than an adjustment, and the eight limitations of Section 14 are honestly bounded rather than hidden.

What remains open is narrow: the exact embedding degree, the largest prime factor of the twist cofactor, and the complete factorisation of N − 1. None of these affects the security conclusions, and all of them are stated as bounds in this document. The minimality of the constant is no longer among the open questions: it is answered, and the answer is negative.

Appendix D. The security ceiling and the cost of the field

This appendix collects, in one place, the two statements that Section 9.9 makes in prose: what the security ceiling of the curve is, and what it costs to carry it. The ceiling is a property of the group order alone. Three of these deserve a comment. The trace-zero two-dimensional lattice attack is the route that genuinely damages other curves in the Koblitz family: on a supersingular curve of trace zero the endomorphism ring has rank four and the Frobenius is not public, so the group can be attacked with a two-dimensional lattice at roughly the square root of a square root. Section 8 established that this curve is ordinary, that EndFp(E) = Z[ω] has rank two, that the trace is non-zero and that the endomorphism is public scalar multiplication by λ; each of those four facts removes one precondition of that attack. The twist transfer route would move a discrete logarithm from the quadratic twist back to E; Section 6 established that gcd(N, nt) = 1, which makes the transfer impossible regardless of how the twist's cofactor factors. The special-purpose index calculus row is not a closure: it is a statement of the art, and the honest position is that no index-calculus attack against this curve is known and none is excluded by the properties certified here.

An invitation to the research community

The curve this document describes was designed and constructed by its author. The present text is not a description of somebody else's curve: it is an attempt to subject a curve of the author's own making to the same treatment the author would demand of anybody else's. Every number in it was produced by the programs printed in Appendix E, from the parameters in Appendix A, and every one of them can be recomputed by a reader with a Python interpreter and nothing else.

That is the reason the author asks the community to take the curve apart.

No claim of novelty or of security in these pages rests on the author's word. The parameters are certified by point order and the Hasse window; the family of six isomorphism classes is certified one class at a time; the endomorphism, the lattice and the exact decomposition are certified arithmetically; the absences, the rational torsion that is not there, the small factors that are not there, the twist that cannot be used for a transfer, are certified the same way. What a reader is being offered, therefore, is not a claim but a set of artifacts to attack, and the author would rather be corrected than believed.

The invitation is specific. Four kinds of contribution would all be useful, and the first two are the ones the author most wants.

  • Find the errors. The open items of Section 14 are a list of things this document does not know: the largest prime factor of the cofactor of the twist order, the exact value of the embedding degree, the complete factorisation of N − 1, the minimality of the choice of c at 2560 bits, and a compiled implementation. Any of them that is settled by a reader settles it for everybody, and any contradiction with the tables in this document is worth more than a confirmation of them.
  • Attack the curve. The argument of Section 9 and Appendix D claims that every descent route is closed and that the ceiling is reached only by the generic discrete logarithm. That claim rests on the endomorphism ring having rank two over the integers, on the curve being ordinary rather than trace zero, on the absence of rational two- and three-torsion, on the coprimality of the order with the order of the twist, and on the embedding degree being large. A reader who believes one of those five is wrong has something to say, and a reader who can find a fourth generator, a rational subgroup, a pairing-friendly structure or an index-calculus improvement has something better.
  • Improve the construction. The recipe of Section 10 is a search over a one-parameter family with a primality condition and a sextic-family condition, and it reproduces the original 256-bit member of the family exactly as a side effect. A larger search, a different seed, a different shape of prime, or a proof that the recipe can be run in reverse from a target order are all natural continuations.
  • Reproduce and re-measure. Two commands in Appendix E regenerate the curve and the 256-bit control case. A compiled implementation, a benchmark on real hardware, and a second implementation in another language would replace the indicative timings of Section 11 with measurements that can be compared.

Two things are worth saying about how the author will treat whatever comes back. First, a certified contradiction will be published as an erratum, in the same document, with the artifact that produces it attached, exactly as the errors listed in Section 13 are published here; the author does not intend to defend a claim that fails an independent check. Second, an improvement that makes the document more accurate is not a criticism of it. The document exists in order to be corrected, and the only outcome in which it has done its job is one in which somebody else has found something the author had missed.

The reference points for any of this are the artifacts of Appendix C and the program of Appendix E. They are shipped with the document so that a reader never has to take a second-hand number on trust.
